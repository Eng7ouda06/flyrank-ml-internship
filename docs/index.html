Ranking Pages That Under-Capture Clicks

Mahmoud Abdelwahab Shaaban (7ouda) · FlyRank ML Internship capstone · Lane: CTR / Engagement Opportunity Scoring

Abstract

Which visible pages should be reviewed first because they earn fewer clicks than peers at the same ranking position? Using the FlyRank starter release (15,237 eligible pages from 29 clients), I labelled a page as under-capturing when its last-30-day CTR fell in the bottom third of its position bucket. I predicted that from prior-30-day signals and static metadata with a gradient-boosted model, validated on held-out clients. It reached ROC AUC 0.82 versus 0.735 for a prior-CTR rule, with 87% precision in its top 5%. The result is observed and predictive, not causal, and supports review prioritisation only.

1. Introduction

Content teams cannot review every page. The decision supported: which already-visible pages deserve a metadata, content or monitoring review first.

2. Data

FlyRank anonymized starter release, one table of 30,000 content items (hashed IDs; no client names, domains, URLs or queries). Kept pages with ≥100 impressions in both the last and previous 30 days and average position ≤ 50 (15,237 rows). Excluded the rest because CTR is too noisy at low impressions. Not the full warehouse release.

3. Methodology

  • Label: last-30d CTR ≤ the 33rd percentile within its position bucket (1–3, 3–10, 10–20, 20–50). Base rate 37.4%.
  • Features: previous-30d impressions and CTR (plus its within-bucket rank), average position, search volume, competition, CPC, word count, content age, days since update, content type, intent.
  • Leakage control: no 90-day CTR, clicks, sessions or trend fields (they contain the label window); client IDs used only for grouping.
  • Baseline: rank by low previous-30d CTR within position bucket.
  • Validation: GroupKFold(5) by client_id, so no client appears in both train and test; seed 42.

5. Limitations

  • Starter slice, not the full warehouse; results may differ on the full release.
  • Average position is a 90-day figure that partly overlaps the label window; this is a directional, not clean-time, split.
  • The label is relative (peer-bottom-third), so it always flags some pages.
  • Associations are observed. This does not show causal effects of metadata changes or anything about Google's algorithm.
